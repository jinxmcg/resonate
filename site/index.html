Independent research / 2026

A structure
for knowledge.

A research graph representation, measured for link prediction and missing-fact prediction, for multi-hop graph queries whose cost is O(1) in depth, and, with a separately trained retrieval pipeline around it, for natural-language questions with no generative model at query time.

RESONATERESEARCH PREVIEW

01 / What we found

One representation supports several operations

We trained the representation to predict links. Then we measured what else it could do. The graph operations use the link-trained representation as it is. Language did need more: the representation was continued on edges and training questions together, and a separately trained retrieval pipeline was built around it.

The surpriseThe same learned representation turned out to be useful for more than the task it was trained for. Once continued on questions, it answers written questions with the same readout that scores a graph edge, and it got better at links while learning to do so.

In plain words: we taught a compact model to guess which facts are missing from a medical knowledge graph. It then turned out to also answer questions typed in English, and to answer long chained questions as fast as short ones, without a chatbot anywhere in the loop.

02 / Evidence

Three benchmarks, three findings

Stanford's Open Graph Benchmark link-prediction task, scored by the official Evaluator: the true answer is ranked among 500 sampled alternatives per query, so MRR is a rank statistic, not a percentage of questions answered.

The STaRK rows are one frozen pipeline read once per split; four such reads happened over the project and all four are listed below. The RotatE control is one seed. FindingCompetitive performance from a compact representation. The bare model beats the official RotatE, ComplEx and DistMult baselines (0.7989, 0.8095, 0.8043) at a seventh of their parameters; the recipe adds 0.037 on top.
FindingMost of a trained entity table is removable. Grouping entities by training degree and replacing each group’s rows with a narrow coefficient vector over a small bank of shared subspaces — k-means plus a per-cluster PCA, about ten seconds, no retraining — takes row C from 27.1M to 9.56M parameters for 0.0061 MRR. The four graph-read members absorb 57% of the loss: the narrow table gives up 0.0143 on its own scores but only 0.0061 after blending, because those features read the training graph rather than the table. The cost is steady across seeds (0.0056–0.0067 over ten, one mild outlier), so it is a property of the trained table rather than a lucky draw. The same construction collapses on wikikg2, where 2.5M near-isotropic rows leave no shared narrow subspace to project onto — the method needs a graph whose rows cluster.
What 9.56M means to serveThe compact row ships as a 39 MB file — 38.2 MB of fp32 weights, 19.1 MB at half precision. The entries it sits between on the board carry 188M parameters, which is 750 MB resident in fp32: 19.6× more memory to answer the same query. Row C, at 27.1M, is 108 MB. Nothing here is sharded or paged — the whole index fits in the memory a single conventional embedding table needs, and rebuilding an entity row is one small matmul (coef @ P + mu) before the relation operator applies. For speed at this shape: the ten released 27.1M checkpoints re-score the entire test split — 162,870 queries in each direction, 500 candidates each — in 40 seconds on one RTX 5090, about 4 seconds per model, and the compact tables are re-scored the same way by verify_compact.py.

FindingThe same representation can be reached from language through a pretrained encoder with a learned head. Continued on edges and training questions, it answers a question from the text alone at 26.7 ± 0.2 Hit@1 (three seeds), its sensitivity to phrasing is under one point, and its link MRR rose from 0.557 to 0.568 while it learned. The pipeline around it runs no generative model.

In plain words: nobody types a database query. You type the question, a learned reader (a pretrained encoder with three small heads) turns it into "which thing, which relation", the graph and the model answer, and no chatbot is consulted.

03 / Examples

What the queries look like

Four kinds of use, each with real, unedited output. The oracle labels are the graph's: [ok] means a supporting path exists in the training graph, which can itself be incomplete, outdated or wrong; [NOVEL] means the graph holds no such edge, an unverified prediction and not a discovery. How much a [NOVEL] answer is worth is measured, not assumed: on 598,543 held-out Wikidata facts the model's score separates a true unseen fact from a random false claim at AUROC 0.98, and from the most plausible false alternative at 0.71.

2. Absorbing new knowledge in minutes (DRKG)

Knowledge graphs go stale and retraining is the usual price. Fine-tuning the 496 held-out treats edges into a trained model took 1,500 steps, 223 seconds of wall-clock on a single laptop GPU, and absorbed all of them to rank 1 (MRR 0.147 → 1.000), with a worst regression elsewhere of −0.008. That closes a loop: the model proposes a [NOVEL] edge, a caller corroborates it against literature, a minutes-long fine-tune absorbs it.

04 / The controlled experiment

Is it just any good embedding?

Maybe any similarly accurate knowledge-graph embedding would take the language projection equally well. We tested that: RotatE trained on the same graph at the same width (288 numbers per entity), under the same regime, tuned by held-out link quality only, and the identical projector trained on top of both.

FindingNearly equal link-prediction quality did not produce equal downstream retrieval. The ResonatE table took the same projection 4 points better frozen and 6 points better jointly, and joint training raised its link quality four times as much.

Caveats, stated with the result: one seed each, one dataset, RotatE's L2 variant, the same training recipe for both. Our first pass used an untuned RotatE and showed a two-fold gap; that number is retracted in the record and the tuned one stands. We do not claim to know why the difference exists.

05 / What produced these results

The architecture

The representation

Each entity is a unit-norm vector of k² complex numbers: k=12 (144 numbers) on biokg and PrimeKG, k=8 (64) on wikikg2. Each relation and direction is a block-diagonal operator, unitary at initialisation and free afterwards: 36 blocks of 4×4 on biokg, one dense 64×64 block on wikikg2. A hop applies the operator and renormalises. The score of a triple is an inner product against the raw target row.

Because the per-hop renormalisation is a scalar, a chain of operators compiles into one operator, and a multi-hop question is one inner-product search over the entity rows. That is what "O(1) in depth" means: the readout is linear in the number of entities, or sublinear through an fp16 HNSW index (recall@10 0.993–0.994); the cost in chain depth is constant.

The training shell and the recipe

The entity table is stored through its real view and read with a sparse lookup, so a step touches only the rows in the batch and the optimiser keeps one number per row (row-wise Adagrad): 21× faster per step than dense Adam at identical quality, 2.5M entities in 36 minutes on one GPU.

The recipe, each lever measured with ten seeds: label-free retrieval members read from the training graph (+0.031 on biokg), distillation at training time from ten seeds at temperature 2 (+0.007), two capacity dials set on validation, and for language a fine-tuned text ranker, a learned parser head and a logistic reranker fit on train with out-of-fold features.

Total rented compute for the two OGB boards: $52.93, every RTX 5090 hour over three days at $0.60 per hour. The STaRK pipeline's own training is about an hour on one RTX 5090: the table (50k steps, ~4 min), its continuation on edges and questions (~5 min), the text-ranker fine-tune (~2 min), the parser head (~4 min), the reranker (seconds), and five out-of-fold refits of the continued table and parser head for honest reranker features. The offline 7B augmentation (paraphrases of the training questions, weak labels for the parser) is on top of that.

06 / What we can and cannot conclude

Observed, and not established

Observed

  • Strong link prediction on two OGB boards; on the synthesized STaRK splits, first on Hit@5, Recall@20 and MRR and second on Hit@1 to pipelines that run a 70B–120B language model per question; well behind those pipelines on the 98 human questions.
  • A compact representation on biokg: 27M parameters, above the 188M official baselines.
  • Missing-link completion: true unseen facts separated from random false ones at AUROC 0.98, from the most plausible false alternative at 0.71; 496 new facts absorbed to rank 1 in 223 s on a laptop GPU.
  • Direct access to the graph, O(1) in depth: compiled chains at 0.08–0.11 ms for depth 1–6; for these graph queries the fp16 table or index is the whole deployable; an exact oracle labels every answer graph-supported or unverified. This describes the graph operations, not the language pipeline.
  • Natural-language retrieval with no generative model at query time, through a separately trained pipeline around the representation: two pretrained 110M encoders with learned heads, a text ranker, a graph walk and a logistic reranker.
  • Better STaRK transfer than the tested, tuned RotatE control.

Not established

  • A new geometry. The operator family is close to existing ones and is not the claim.
  • Why ResonatE transfers to language better than the control.
  • That the language pipeline and the representation form one model. They do not: the pipeline is a separate stack trained around the table. A shared encoder for both is an untested proposal.
  • Universality across graphs and tasks: three graphs, one seed on the control.
  • That LLMs are unnecessary for arbitrary knowledge reasoning. This is retrieval over one graph.
  • That the architecture is optimal, or compact on large graphs: on wikikg2 it is not.
  • An intersection operator. "Drugs that target X and treat Y" still needs the exact graph walk; two pre-registered attempts to put the AND into the representation failed.

In plain words: we know what it does because we measured it. We do not yet know why it does some of it better than a standard alternative, and we do not claim it can reason.

Negative results, changed hypotheses, corrections

  • RotatE control, first pass retracted. An untuned RotatE showed a two-fold gap on the language projection; tuning it by link quality closed most of that. The tuned gap of 4–6 Hit@1 is what we report.
  • Two failed attempts at an AND. A query-time intersection readout (min, soft-min, product, soft count) scored below a plain sum; training the representation on 30,000 sampled conjunctions doubled its scores on held-out conjunctions but the sum still won. Both were pre-registered with predictions that turned out wrong on the level.
  • Real versus complex operators: no difference (0.7923 vs 0.7897 on the validation screen). Block size: flat above 4×4. Fourteen rejected levers are documented in the paper.
  • STaRK: a higher-scoring read rejected, and three levers dropped. P4 added a learned selector over four readings of each question and scored 43.7 on the synthesized test split against P3's 43.1, but lost five of 98 human questions; it is a bolt fit to the synthesized templates and was rejected. Three further pre-registered levers stayed on validation or train and were dropped: alias matching for symbols (−0.2), a text ranker fine-tuned with terse paraphrases (no gain over three seeds), and P5, latent-confirmed anchors that let the question vector disambiguate same-name entities, which lost 1 to 7 Hit@1 at every threshold on train and never reached validation.
  • Two wikikg2 rows not filed. The learned combiner is fit on validation labels; OGB's rule excludes that. Reported with receipts, not entered.
  • Every development test read is listed, including the three runs of a non-submitted row read twice and the four reads of the STaRK test splits (P1 to P4, all in the table above).

The whole story in one paragraph: we built a compact knowledge representation to predict links. It performed well. We then found that the same representation supports direct access as it is, and natural-language retrieval once continued on questions and wrapped in a separately trained pipeline of pretrained encoders, a graph walk and a reranker. We ran a control to see whether that property was generic, and it was not entirely. We do not yet claim to know why.
