\begin{abstract}
We report three results on \texttt{ogbl-biokg}, the Open Graph
Benchmark's biomedical link-prediction task, each as the mean and
unbiased standard deviation of ten independent runs with one test
evaluation per run under the official Evaluator.
(A) A 27.1M-parameter embedding in which entities are complex vectors of
144 coefficients and each of the 102 (relation, direction) operators is
a set of 36 independent $4\times4$ complex blocks initialised unitary
reaches test MRR $0.8118\pm0.0013$, above ComplEx, DistMult and RotatE
at 188M parameters.
(B) Four label-free retrieval features computed from the training graph
alone---the similarity, under the model's own embeddings or under raw
edge overlap, between the query entity and the training entities that
already hold the candidate edge---blended with the model by a
per-relation weight selected on validation, reach $0.8425\pm0.0007$.
(C) A fresh 27.1M model distilled at training time from the ten models
of (A), with its own features, reaches $0.8505\pm0.0004$, which would
place fifth of twelve on the public leaderboard as of September 2026 and
fourth among single models, at the smallest parameter count on it. The
recipe was found by a pre-registered screen-then-confirm search on the
validation split in which fourteen of sixteen candidate levers were
rejected, several because the architecture already supplies what the
lever would add; we report them. Code, logs, frozen blend weights and
checkpoints are released; the per-run test receipts reproduce
deterministically from the released caches.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Knowledge-graph embedding models score a triple $(h,r,t)$ by a function
of learned vectors, and on \texttt{ogbl-biokg} \citep{hu2020ogb} the
classic bilinear and rotational families (DistMult, ComplEx, RotatE)
sit near test MRR 0.80--0.81 at roughly 188M parameters, while the
current leaders reach 0.85--0.86 through learned scoring functions,
auxiliary objectives or relation-aware ensembles
\citep{ogbl_biokg_leaderboard}. This report describes a model with a
different inductive bias and a different budget---27.1M parameters---and
two label-free additions that lift it from 0.812 to 0.851 without adding
a single parameter fitted to anything but the training triples.

The model, ResonatE, was designed for multi-hop reasoning: an entity is
a unit-norm complex vector, a relation is a linear operator on that
space, a hop is ``apply the operator and renormalise'', and a chain of
hops is a chain of operators. \texttt{ogbl-biokg} is a single-edge task,
so here only the one-hop score matters, but the design leaves marks on
the results: unit-norm renormalisation after every hop makes the usual
N3 regulariser redundant, type-grouped batches make a
relation-prediction auxiliary loss redundant, and exact operator
composition makes path training redundant---each verified negatively in
Section~\ref{sec:negative}.

The two additions are (i) \emph{retrieval features}: for a candidate
answer $c$ to a query $(h,r,?)$, look up the training entities that
already hold $(\cdot,r,c)$ and score $c$ by how similar they are to $h$,
either in the model's own embedding space or by raw edge overlap
(Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}); and (ii) \emph{distillation at training
time}: a fresh model whose loss includes the KL divergence to the mean
prediction of ten frozen models on each batch's own candidate set
(Section~\ref{sec:distill}). Neither reads validation or test edges. The
blend weights of (i) are selected on validation from a fixed list of at
most nine candidate vectors per relation and direction; the selection is
reported alongside its held-out estimate, which is within $0.001$ of test
on every one of the twenty runs.

Our contributions are the three ten-run results, the recipe search with
its negative results, the retrieval-feature construction, and a fully
scripted reproduction path with released checkpoints. The operator
family itself is not a contribution; the next paragraph places it.

\paragraph{Relation to prior operator families.}
Representing a relation as a rotation or a small orthogonal block, so
that mixed-relation chains compose non-commutatively, is an established
line: RotatE \citep{sun2019rotate} uses $1\times1$ complex rotations
(commuting), QuatE \citep{zhang2019quate} and DensE \citep{lu2022dense}
quaternion rotations, Rotate3D \citep{gao2020rotate3d} rotations in
$\mathbb{R}^3$, OrthogonalE \citep{zhu2024orthogonale} block-diagonal
orthogonal matrices with Riemannian optimisation and matrix-valued
entities, and Knowledgebra \citep{yang2022knowledgebra} free matrix
semigroups. ResonatE's operator is nearest to OrthogonalE: block-diagonal
with small complex blocks, unitary at initialisation---but the
constraint is dropped afterwards (the blocks are free), the query side
is renormalised after every hop while the target side keeps its norm as
a popularity channel, and no path or composition loss is used
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}). We make no claim that this operator is
new or better than those families; on this benchmark the block size
moves validation MRR by $+0.0055$ ($2\times2\to4\times4$, 50k steps)
and $8\times8$ is flat against $4\times4$ at the screen (0.7904 vs
0.7893, Section~\ref{sec:recipe}), which is the size of the effect the
operator form has here. The project's name and the
``mode'' vocabulary in the code are a coordinate label: the score is
invariant under any block-diagonal unitary change of basis
($E\to UE$, $H_r\to UH_rU^\dagger$), no Fourier transform is taken
anywhere, and nothing in the results depends on the coordinates being
spectral. Nor is ``complex'' a claim. The one testable residue of the
complex form is its parameter sharing: a complex $4\times4$ block on
$\C^{144}$ and a free real $4\times4$ block on $\mathbb{R}^{288}$ both
have 1{,}152 real coefficients per relation and the same 27{,}124{,}129
parameters in total. Pre-registered and run on the 12.5k-step validation
screen with two seeds each, the real model scores 0.7923 against the
complex model's 0.7897, and a real $8\times8$ block (which contains the
complex $4\times4$ as a special case) 0.7898; the difference is below
the 0.005 we had fixed as the smallest meaningful gap and its sign is
against the complex form. The complex arithmetic is a way of writing a
real block model on $\mathbb{R}^{2M}$, and the released code accepts
\texttt{real=True} to write it the other way.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Entities and relations.}
Let $N$ be the number of entities and $M=k^2$ with $k=12$, so $M=144$.
The entity table is $E\in\C^{N\times M}$. A query entity is read through
$\cnorm(E_h)=E_h/\lVert E_h\rVert$; a target entity is read as the raw
row $E_t$, so target norms are free and act as a learned per-entity
popularity channel. For each of the $2R$ (relation, direction) pairs
($R=51$, both directions trained as separate operators, $2R=102$) the
operator $H_r$ is block-diagonal with $M/b$ independent $b\times b$
complex blocks, $b=4$, initialised as random unitaries by QR
decomposition and then trained freely. A hop is
\begin{equation}
  z = \cnorm(H_r\,\cnorm(E_h)),
\end{equation}
and the score of candidate $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  s(h,r,t) = \Rel\langle z, E_t\rangle\cdot e^{\tau},
\end{equation}
with a single learned log-temperature $\tau$. Blocks larger than
$1\times1$ do not commute, so mixed-relation chains are representable;
$b=4$ was chosen on validation (Section~\ref{sec:recipe}).

\paragraph{Parameter count.}
$N\cdot M$ complex entity coefficients ($93{,}773\times144$), $2R\cdot
(M/b)\cdot b^2$ complex operator coefficients ($102\times36\times16$) and
$\tau$: counting each complex coefficient as two reals, as the
leaderboard does for ComplEx, $27{,}124{,}129$ parameters. The literal
number of parameter elements is $13{,}562{,}065$.

\paragraph{Training.}
Entity ids are per type in OGB; we flatten them with per-type offsets so
that each relation's head and tail types map to contiguous ranges. A
training batch is 2{,}048 triples of a single relation (relations
sampled in proportion to their frequency), scored in one random
direction against a single shared set of 4{,}096 negatives drawn
uniformly from the target type's range---the same candidate distribution
the Evaluator uses. The loss is the sampled softmax cross-entropy over
$[\text{positive}\mid\text{negatives}]$ plus a trajectory term
$0.1\,\lVert z-E_t\rVert^2$ on the hopped state, with gradient-norm
clipping at 1. Adam \citep{kingma2015adam} at learning rate
$5\cdot10^{-3}$ with cosine decay \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr} for 50{,}000
steps ($\approx21$ epochs). Training takes about four minutes on an RTX
5090 and eighteen on a GTX 1080 Ti.

\section{Retrieval features from the training graph}
\label{sec:retrieval}

The Evaluator scores each query $(h,r,?)$ over 501 candidates: the true
tail and 500 fixed, type-matched negatives (and symmetrically for
heads). For every candidate $c$ we form features from the
\emph{holders} of $c$ under $r$ in the training graph,
$\mathcal{H}_r(c)=\{h' : (h',r,c)\in\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}\}$, and
the similarity between the query entity $h$ and those holders:
\begin{align}
  \text{analogy}(h,r,c) &= \max_{h'\in\mathcal{H}_r(c)}
     \cos(\cnorm(E_h),\cnorm(E_{h'}))^{3}, \\
  \text{jaccard}(h,r,c) &= \max_{h'\in\mathcal{H}_r(c)}
     \frac{|\mathcal{N}(h)\cap\mathcal{N}(h')|}{|\mathcal{N}(h)\cup\mathcal{N}(h')|},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{N}(\cdot)$ is the typed training-edge set of an entity,
plus the two ``top-3'' variants that replace the max by the mean of the
three largest similarities. The cube on the cosine sharpens the
analogy score; it is the only shape choice and was made on validation.
A candidate with no holders gets the minimum score. Both families read
training edges only---validation and test edges are never looked
up---so they are as label-free as the embedding itself; the Jaccard
pair does not use the model at all. This is case-based reasoning in
the model's own geometry (analogy) and in the raw graph (Jaccard): the
embedding compresses an entity's neighbourhood at training time, the
graph remembers it exactly at query time. Alone, on validation, the
four features reach MRR 0.645 (analogy), 0.751 (analogy top-3), 0.671
(Jaccard) and 0.764 (Jaccard top-3).

\paragraph{Blend.}
For each member (the model plus the four features) the 501 candidate
scores of a query are $z$-normalised. The blended score is a weighted
sum with one weight vector $w\in\Delta^{4}$ per (relation, direction)
group. The candidate set for a group is fixed: uniform, the uniform mean
of the top-1, -3, -5 or -9 members by that group's validation MRR, and
$\operatorname{softmax}(\eta\cdot\text{MRR})$ for $\eta\in\{20,50,100\}$;
the choice is made hierarchically---a global vector first, then one per
relation family (the \texttt{drug-drug\_*} and
\texttt{protein-protein\_*} relations pooled), then one per relation,
each level adding the parent's choice to its candidate list and a group
only receiving its own weights if it has at least 4{,}000 validation
rows. This is a selection among at most nine vectors per group, not a
gradient fit. Its optimism is measured by the same procedure with the
weights fit on a random half of the validation triples (a triple's two
directions stay together) and evaluated on the other half
(\texttt{ensemble\_weights.py}); the committed test shot refits on the
full validation split and applies the frozen weights once
(\texttt{freeze\_test.py}). The frozen weights are released with each
receipt.

\section{Distillation at training time}
\label{sec:distill}

Ten checkpoints are a deployment cost, and a uniform score average of
the ten row-A models reaches only $0.8428$ (one test shot, retired).
Instead we train a fresh model of the same architecture and recipe
whose loss adds, on every batch,
\begin{equation}
  \lambda\,T^2\,\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\operatorname{softmax}(\bar\ell/T)\,\big\|\,
  \operatorname{softmax}(\ell/T)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\ell$ are the student's logits over the batch's
$[\text{positive}\mid4{,}096\text{ negatives}]$ and $\bar\ell$ the mean
of the ten frozen teachers' logits on the same candidates
\citep{hinton2015distilling}, with $\lambda=1$, $T=1$. The teachers see
only the training triples the student sees; the student runs alone at
inference. Ten students (seeds 0--9, the same ten teachers) reach
validation MRR $0.8287\pm0.0006$ alone---$+0.016$ over their
teachers---and $0.8505\pm0.0004$ on test with their own retrieval
features.

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Data and metric.}
\texttt{ogbl-biokg}: 93{,}773 entities of five types (disease 10{,}687,
drug 10{,}533, function 45{,}085, protein 17{,}499, side effect 9{,}969),
51 relations, 4{,}762{,}678 training, 162{,}886 validation and 162{,}870
test triples. Filtered MRR over 500 type-matched negatives per direction
via \texttt{ogb.linkproppred.Evaluator} (\texttt{ogb} 1.3.6). Our
loader asserts that the three splits are pairwise disjoint as loaded
(\texttt{verify.py}).

\paragraph{Pre-registration and test reads.}
Every configuration's expected test range was written in the project
log before its first test read. All comparisons between recipes,
features, blend rules and distillation settings were made on validation.
The test split is read once per run: row A by the trainer's final
evaluation, rows B and C by the committed blend shot; test score caches
are written without computing any per-member test metric. Nine test
reads were made during development outside the three rows, each a
single shot of a configuration chosen on validation and none re-run:
the 25k-step baseline (0.8024); 50k steps with $2\times2$ blocks, seeds
0 and 1 (0.8084, 0.8080); the final recipe's seeds 0 and 1 trained on
the 1080 Ti (0.8134, 0.8123; their 5090 retrains are row A seeds 0 and
1, 0.8135 and 0.8130); the uniform
ten-model ensemble (0.8428); seed 0 with three analogy features
(0.8368); a 48M single model ($k=16$, 16{,}384 negatives, per-entity
bias) with its features (0.8472); and the same 48M recipe distilled from
twelve checkpoints, with features (0.8535). The last two are exploratory
and are not part of any reported row.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Mean $\pm$ unbiased standard deviation (\texttt{torch.std}) over seeds
0--9. Row C's ten students share the same ten teachers, so its
deviation reflects student-seed variance only.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Rows A--C were trained on rented RTX 5090 (32~GB) instances with
torch 2.11.0 (CUDA 12.8); the retrieval features of seeds 7--9 of row B
and all verification ran on a GTX 1080 Ti (11~GB) with torch 2.6.0
(CUDA 12.4).
Checkpoints trained on one machine re-evaluate on the other to within
$3\cdot10^{-4}$ MRR. The analogy features need about 6~GB of RAM and
25--40 minutes per split per seed on one CPU core.

\subsection{How the recipe was found}
\label{sec:recipe}

The starting point was a hand-picked recipe from smaller graphs: $k=12$,
$2\times2$ blocks, 4{,}096 negatives, learning rate $5\cdot10^{-3}$
cosine, 25{,}000 steps. Each default was then treated as a hypothesis
under a tiered protocol written down before each run: a 12{,}500-step
\emph{screen} on validation, promotion only above the current recipe by
$+0.004$ MRR, then a 50{,}000-step \emph{confirmation}. Sixteen levers
went through it; two survived.

\paragraph{Budget.} Validation MRR at 6{,}250 / 12{,}500 / 25{,}000 /
50{,}000 steps (each with its own cosine schedule) is 0.7351 / 0.7829 /
0.8032 / 0.8085: the inherited budget had not plateaued. The gain per
doubling shrinks fast enough that 100k projects at $\le+0.002$, so the
budget was doubled once and frozen.

\paragraph{Capacity: blocks, not embedding width.} Capacity can go into $k$
(every entity's embedding grows; millions of parameters) or into the
relation blocks ($2\times2\to4\times4$; 118k real parameters in total). In
2{,}000-step probes validation MRR grows log-linearly in $k$ with no
plateau (Table~\ref{tab:capacity}), but the gain compresses with budget:
$k=16$'s $+0.025$ at 2k steps is $+0.003$ at 50k, and even $k=32$ (192M
parameters) misses the promotion bar. Blocks are the opposite: their
$+0.0064$ screen gain barely compresses to $+0.0055$ at 50k
(0.8140)---expressivity rather than capacity, at roughly $1/300$ the
parameter price per MRR point. Stacking $k=16$ on $4\times4$ blocks adds
almost nothing; the two knobs buy overlapping capacity. Uniform
$8\times8$ blocks are flat (0.7904 at the screen vs 0.7893).

\subsection{What did not work}
\label{sec:negative}

The remaining fourteen levers---mostly the tricks other leaderboard
entries rely on---went through the same screen-then-confirm pipeline on
top of the adopted recipe (Table~\ref{tab:negative}; the 12{,}500-step
reference is that recipe's own 0.7893). Two patterns account for them.
First, \emph{the architecture pre-empts the trick}: a relation-prediction
auxiliary loss \citep{chen2021relation} is monotonically harmful because
type-grouped batches already force the model to separate relations; N3
regularisation \citep{lacroix2018canonical} has a real but tiny interior
optimum because renormalising after every hop already disciplines
magnitudes; compositional path training \citep{guu2015traversing} has
nothing to repair because hops compose exactly by construction and the
chains only spend the update budget. Second, \emph{screen-tier wins
vanish at the plateau}: more negatives and tied reverse operators both
pass the 12{,}500-step screen and are flat or negative at 50k---they
speed convergence without raising the ceiling. A one-cycle schedule
loses to cosine, and phase-only (unitary) relation operators never reach
90\% accuracy at any depth in the synthetic chain testbed: the moduli
off the unit circle are load-bearing.

\subsection{Retrieval and distillation, step by step}

Table~\ref{tab:shots} lists the single-seed shots that led to rows B and
C. Each configuration was chosen on validation and read on test once.
Three analogy features on top of seed 0 give 0.8368; adding the Jaccard
pair lifts the held-out estimate to 0.8441 (with analogy features that
borrowed three seeds' embeddings); a model with its \emph{own} four
features gives 0.8433---above the uniform ten-model ensemble's 0.8428
with one model. The two rows that go on the board are these last two
recipes re-run as ten-seed protocols.

Two levers rejected in Section~\ref{sec:negative} returned once the
recipe was stronger: 16{,}384 negatives and $k=16$ are each flat alone
but reach 0.8171 validation together, and a per-entity score bias adds
$+0.003$ on top; that 48M model with its features gives 0.8472, and
distilled from twelve checkpoints 0.8535. We did not run them as
ten-seed rows. What did not help, all measured held-out before any
shot: $k=24$ (parity at twice the parameters), a confidence gate on the
blend weights ($-0.0015$), cross-relation neighbourhood overlap
($+0.0002$), compositional training ($-0.005$).

\paragraph{Where the errors are.} On the rarest relations the lookup
beats the embedding outright: on side effects with at most 19 training
edges the analogy feature alone reaches held-out MRR 0.0175 against the
27M model's 0.0147 (blend 0.0196; paired bootstrap significant). Of the
remaining errors 36\% are drug$\to$side-effect tails, where even a
22-member ensemble scores 0.17---a floor set by the data. Across the ten
seeds the truth-versus-runner-up margin has a constant spread of 0.28
and only its mean moves: seeds disagree on which near-tied candidate
wins, not on what they know, which is why more seeds were never going to
close the gap and why the deployable unit is one table plus a lookup.

\section{Beyond the single-edge task}
\label{sec:beyond}

The leaderboard credential is a one-hop score; the architecture was
built for chains, and the same code trained on graphs with multi-hop
questions shows the properties the benchmark cannot. We summarise them
briefly; they are not part of the submission.

\paragraph{Depth.} On synthetic worlds with exact ground truth at every
depth, models trained only on chains of depth $\le4$ ($k=8$, 25k steps,
three seeds, 2{,}000 chains per depth) are read out to depth 48. Three
results, in decreasing order of what they show. \emph{One relation
applied $n$ times}, on 1{,}000 entities with seven random permutations
as relations: the diagonal model holds 0.987 at depth 8 and crosses 90\%
between depth 10 and 12 and 50\% between 16 and 20; the $4\times4$-block
model 0.963 at depth 8. This is a statement about powers of a single
operator, not about composition. \emph{A different relation at every
hop}, so that order matters, on the same random permutations: neither the
block model nor a dense $64\times64$ operator learns depth 2 (0.21 and
0.65 accuracy, $\approx0$ beyond), because random permutations share no
low-dimensional structure; the block model does not compose arbitrary
relations. \emph{A different relation at every hop on relations that
form a group}: a turtle on a $16\times16$ torus with forward, back, turn
left and right, strafe left and right, and U-turn, i.e.\ 1{,}024
(heading, $x$, $y$) states and rigid motions generating
$\mathbb{Z}_{16}^2\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_4$, whose irreducible representations
are at most four-dimensional. Half of all depth-2 chains change their
answer when reordered, and a commuting diagonal model reaches 0.75 at
depth 2. The $4\times4$-block model trained on depth $\le4$ reaches 0.949
at depth 8, crosses 90\% between depth 10 and 12 and 50\% between 20 and
24 (seed s.d.\ $\le0.003$ to depth 8); the dense operator reaches
further (0.996 at depth 8, 0.899 at depth 20), so the block size is a
ceiling rather than the mechanism. The allowed statement is therefore
that the model composes non-commuting relations beyond its trained depth
when the relations form a group with a low-dimensional representation,
and not for arbitrary permutations; whether the relations of a real graph
are closer to the former or the latter is not measured. Both
mixed-relation experiments were pre-registered after a first external
review asked for them, and the earlier single-relation number was the
only one we had quoted. Mapping natural-language questions onto chains
of relation operators is outside this report.

\paragraph{Latency and a compiled index.} Because the per-hop
renormalisation is a scalar, a metapath's operator chain collapses into
one block-diagonal operator (2{,}304 bytes for the $2\times2$ models),
and the readout is a real inner product---maximum-inner-product search
over a real $N\times2M$ table. On DRKG \citep{ioannidis2020drkg}
(97{,}238 nodes, 5.87M edges, a 28.1M-parameter model) batched
multi-hop queries take 0.08--0.11~ms at every depth 1--6 while exact
traversal grows with fan-out, up to $45\times$ slower at depth 5--6;
compiled scores equal hop-by-hop scores to $10^{-5}$ with identical
top-10 sets over 1{,}867 queries, and an HNSW index
\citep{malkov2018hnsw,johnson2019faiss} over the table gives recall@10
0.993 against the exact readout. Storing the table in fp16 (848 bytes
per row including HNSW links) leaves the top-10 identical on all 3{,}334
queries over two graphs; int8 costs a little (top-1 agreement 0.98),
int4 breaks retrieval (0.70).

\paragraph{Oracle validation.} On Hetionet \citep{himmelstein2017hetionet}
and DRKG every top answer is checked against an exact graph oracle and
labelled as a citable path or as a novel hypothesis; on the trained
metapath libraries the top answer is oracle-verified 94.6--100\% of the
time across all ten DRKG paths and 99--100\% on all eight Hetionet
paths. Fine-tuning 496 held-out \emph{treats} edges into a trained DRKG
model took 3.7 GPU-minutes, absorbed all of them to rank 1 (MRR
$0.147\to1.000$) with a worst metapath regression of $-0.008$.

\paragraph{Scaling.} A training step touches about 10k rows whatever
$N$ is; what kept the table resident was dense Adam state. Replacing it
for the entity table by row-wise Adagrad \citep{duchi2011adagrad,
lerer2019pbg} (one accumulator per row, no decay, untouched rows left
bit-identical) costs nothing measurable on Hetionet (hits@10 0.782 vs
0.782 over three seeds) and lies within one standard error on DRKG
(link-prediction MRR 0.157 vs 0.163 on 496 edges). From measured
per-row costs, 100M entities at $k=12$ would need a 115~GB fp32
training table, 0.4~GB of optimiser state, an 85~GB fp16 HNSW serving
index (or about 5~GB of RAM plus NVMe in a DiskANN layout
\citep{subramanya2019diskann}) and roughly 7 GPU-hours for 20 epochs of
500M edges on one 5090-class card; the remaining work is sparse
row-gradient plumbing and type-sharding, and none of it has been run.

\section{Limitations and disclosures}

The blend weights of rows B and C are selected on the full validation
split; we report the in-sample validation number and the held-out
estimate of the same selection, which is within $0.001$ of test on all
twenty runs. Row C's ten students share one teacher pool. The retrieval
features cost 25--40 CPU-minutes per split per seed and roughly 6~GB of
RAM, and at inference a query needs the holder lists of its 501
candidates---cheap, but a graph lookup the pure embedding does not
need. Nine development test reads are listed in
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. Training is seeded but CUDA kernels are not
bit-deterministic, so a retrained checkpoint reproduces its MRR to
seed-level noise rather than bit-for-bit; the blend stage is exact. No
external data was used.

\section{Reproducibility}

The repository \url{https://github.com/jinxmcg/resonate} contains the

\texttt{verify.py} that asserts split disjointness and re-evaluates the
